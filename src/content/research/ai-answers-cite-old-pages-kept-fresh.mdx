Refreshing the pages we already have beats publishing new ones — keeping
content updated can be important to maintain citations.

## Summary

- Answer engines seem to be biased to cite old pages that have been recently
  updated instead of fresh new pages.
- The typical cited page is <mark>two years</mark> old. Half were published
  more than two years ago; one in six before 2020.
- <mark>Seven in ten</mark> were edited in the last year. Only four in ten
  were *written* in the last year.
- Plotted by half-year, publish dates are spread across a decade. Last-edit
  dates are almost all at the right edge: <mark>six in ten</mark> fall in 2026.
- ChatGPT cites the most recently edited pages. The two Google surfaces are
  indistinguishable from each other.
- The page cited first tends to be fresher than the pages cited after it.

## Takeaways

### ChatGPT cites the freshest pages

- <mark>55%</mark> of ChatGPT's cited articles were edited in the last 90 days,
  against 39% for both Google surfaces. Median 76 days since edit vs 125.
  (Declared dates only.)
- The two Google surfaces are indistinguishable from each other on every
  freshness measure.
- All three cite articles of the same age since publish — about 440 days. The
  difference is entirely in how recently the page was edited. ChatGPT's live
  search appears to reward the edit; Google's index appears to reward the page.

### The first citation is fresher than the rest

- The page cited in position one had a median edit age of <mark>73 days</mark>;
  every other position, 121 days.
- Small sample (37 first positions with a declared date), so treat this as a
  direction. But it points the same way as the engine result: recency of edit
  goes with prominence, not just with inclusion.

### SaaS is the freshest

Declared dates only.

| Vertical | Median days since created | Median days since edit |
|:--|--:|--:|
| <mark>SaaS</mark> | <mark>497</mark> | <mark>86</mark> |
| Finance | 255 | 125 |
| Local services | 324 | 125 |
| Travel | 517 | 135 |
| <mark>Shopping</mark> | <mark>484</mark> | <mark>125</mark> |

- SaaS pages are the oldest *and* the most recently edited — pricing pages,
  comparison pages and integration catalogues are living documents.
- Shopping is the opposite: two-year-old gift guides and "best of" roundups
  still being cited, a third of them untouched in over a year.
- Finance and local services cite younger pages than the rest.
- Wellness had too few dated citations to report.

### What this changes about the work

- The instinct to publish new pages to get cited is backwards on this data. The
  page that gets cited has existed long enough to be indexed, linked and
  trusted — and was edited recently enough to be current.
- That makes the refresh the unit of work, not the post: revisit the pages that
  already rank, update the facts, and stamp the date.
- This is related to a finding from [the answer-quality
  study](/research/answer-quality-chatgpt-vs-google/): most factual errors in AI
  answers were staleness, not invention. The engines cite maintained pages
  because maintained pages are where the current facts are.

## Method

<Callout>
  <ul>
    <li>59 prompts across six verticals (SaaS, finance, shopping, travel,
    local services, wellness), captured 2026-09-11, signed out, on the free public
    version of each engine. Google pinned to New York; ChatGPT country-level.</li>
    <li>Every cited URL — 733 distinct pages — was fetched once the same day.
    Platform pages that never state a date (Google's own Shopping, Maps and
    search links, YouTube, Reddit, Instagram) are set aside: 198 of them,
    leaving <b>535 pages</b> to date.</li>
    <li><b>Declared dates first.</b> What the page says about itself, in trust
    order: Open Graph <code>article:modified_time</code>, JSON-LD
    <code>dateModified</code>, <code>og:updated_time</code>, plain
    <code>&lt;meta&gt;</code>. Same ladder for the publish date. HTTP
    <code>Last-Modified</code> headers and dates within 24 hours of our fetch
    are discarded (deploy stamps and templates that print "now").</li>
    <li><b>Wayback estimates second.</b> Where a page declares nothing, the
    publish date is its first Wayback Machine capture (an upper bound: the
    page is at least that old) and the last edit is the midpoint of the
    window between the last capture whose prose matches today's page and the
    first that doesn't. Rotating listings (flight routes, directories, deals)
    are pinned to the run date. Every estimate is flagged and drawn striped in
    the chart.</li>
    <li>The headline describes the <b>356 pages</b> with both dates: 215 with
    both declared, the rest with at least one estimated. Of the remaining 179
    pages, 61 had only a publish date, 14 only an edit date, 104 neither
    (never archived, JS-only, PDF, or refused the fetch).</li>
  </ul>
</Callout>

## Caveats

- **Two fifths of the headline set is estimated, and the estimates lean in
  known directions.** 141 of 356 pages carry at least one Wayback-derived date.
  A first-capture date is an upper bound on creation, so estimated publish
  bars lean late: the blue bars are, if anything, too far right, and the pages
  are older than drawn. Last-edit windows have a median width of about four
  months; the midpoint can miss by two. 27 rotating listings are pinned to the
  run date and account for part of the tallest orange bar. Drop them and
  "edited in the last year" moves from 69% to <mark>66%</mark>; the median
  time since edit from 145 to 172 days. The shape survives.
- **The declared dates are the pages' own.** We take each page's created and
  modified markup at its word. A page can overstate freshness (a template that
  re-stamps on every deploy) or understate it (a real edit with no date
  change). The 24-hour and header exclusions catch the loudest overstatement;
  nothing catches the understatement.
- **A third of citations could not be dated at all.** 198 platform pages never
  state dates: Google's own search, Shopping and Maps links (101), YouTube (34),
  Reddit (31), Instagram (9), Facebook (7), plus a handful of app-store and
  LinkedIn links; 104 ordinary pages were never archived, are JS-rendered or PDF,
  or refused the fetch. The headline describes the pages that could be dated.
- **One day's snapshot.** 356 dated pages across 59 prompts. Engine and
  vertical gaps under ~10 points are within noise until they repeat across runs;
  the headline shape — old page, recent edit — is large enough to trust.
- **Correlation, not cause.** Engines may cite fresh pages because they prefer
  freshness, or because the pages worth citing happen to be the ones people
  maintain. This study can't separate the two.
- **Signed-out and free tiers only.** Logged-in or paid surfaces may cite
  differently.

### However, estimates don't seem to change the outcome

- Restrict to the 213 citations whose page declares a usable edit date, with
  nothing estimated, and the numbers move but the shape doesn't: median age
  <mark>15 months</mark>, median time since edit <mark>4 months</mark>.
- Three quarters of those were edited within the year; seven in ten had been
  edited after they were published; the median gap from publish to last edit
  is <mark>419 days</mark>.
- The declared set is fresher than the blended set because publishers who
  mark up dates are the publishers who go back and edit. The Wayback check on
  undated pages (see Method) is what pulls "three quarters" down to "seven in ten".
- The stalest cited article we found was a 2017 travel-blog post never touched
  since. It is the exception; the middle of the distribution is a maintained page.
